    Glossary · Think like you care

    This glossary accompanies the Think Like You Care module. It's not something to memorise, but a resource to explore and return to whenever a word or concept trips you up. Each entry includes a short definition, an example, links to related terms, and the Portuguese equivalent used in the module slides. Suggestions and contributions are always welcome.

    Jump to: A · B · C · D · E · F · G · H · I · L · M · O · P · R · S

    A

    Ad hominem

    A fallacy that attacks the person instead of answering their argument. - Example: "Don't listen to what he says about the climate: he didn't even finish secondary school." - See also: Fallacy - PT: ad hominem

    AI hallucination

    When an AI tool makes up information (a figure, a quote, a source) and presents it with total confidence, as if it were true. - Example: A chatbot recommends a book whose title and author don't exist. - See also: Generative AI - PT: alucinação (IA)

    Algorithm

    A set of rules a program follows to make a decision. On social media, the algorithm chooses what appears in your feed, usually based on what keeps you on the screen. - Example: You watched three cooking videos and now your feed is full of them. - See also: Filter bubble, Attention economy - PT: algoritmo

    Argument

    A set of reasons (premises) that support an idea (a conclusion). - Example: "Not sleeping enough hurts concentration. I have a test tomorrow. So I should go to bed early." - See also: Premise, Conclusion, Evidence - PT: argumento

    Attention economy

    A business model where your attention is the product: the longer you spend on an app, the more money it makes from advertising. That's why content that triggers strong emotions (outrage, fear, surprise) tends to get promoted more. - See also: Algorithm, Emotional reasoning - PT: economia da atenção

    B

    Bandwagon effect

    The tendency to believe or do something because lots of other people do. - Example: "Everyone in class already has this app, so it must be safe." - See also: Cognitive bias - PT: efeito de manada

    Barnum effect

    The tendency to accept vague descriptions that could fit anyone as if they were "made for us". Also called the Forer effect. - Example: A horoscope says "sometimes you're sociable, other times you'd rather be alone", and it feels like it knows you. - See also: Cognitive bias - PT: efeito Barnum

    C

    Cognitive bias

    A mental shortcut that distorts our reasoning without us noticing. Everyone has them: the goal isn't to pretend they don't exist, but to learn to recognise them. - Examples: confirmation bias, bandwagon effect, emotional reasoning, Barnum effect. - PT: viés cognitivo

    Conclusion

    The idea an argument is trying to support. - Example: In "It's raining, so I'll take an umbrella", the conclusion is "I'll take an umbrella". - PT: conclusão

    Confirmation bias

    The tendency to look for, notice and remember mainly the information that confirms what we already think. - Example: We only follow people who think like us and ignore everyone else. - Tip: Ask yourself: "What would make me change my mind?" - See also: Hypothesis, Filter bubble - PT: viés de confirmação

    CRAAP test

    Five questions for evaluating a source:

    Letter Criterion Question PT
    C Currency When was it published? Is it still up to date? Atualidade
    R Relevance Does it actually answer my question? Relevância
    A Authority Who wrote it? Do they know the subject? Autoridade
    A Accuracy Is there evidence? Do other sources confirm it? Precisão
    P Purpose Is it trying to inform, sell, persuade or entertain? Propósito
    • See also: Source, Credibility
    • PT: teste CRAAP

    Credibility

    How much trust a source deserves. It depends on who's writing, what they know about the subject, the evidence they give and their intentions. - Example: An article by a medical specialist, citing studies, has more credibility on health than an anonymous comment. - PT: credibilidade

    Critical thinking

    Analysing information carefully to reach a well-founded judgement. It isn't having the right answer to everything, or being contrary: it's wanting to be well informed, enjoying putting ideas to the test, and being open to changing your mind. - PT: pensamento crítico

    D

    Deepfake

    A fake video, image or audio clip made with AI that looks real. It can make someone appear to say or do something they never said or did. - Example: A video of a public figure announcing something they never announced. - See also: Reverse image search - PT: deepfake

    Disinformation

    False or misleading information created on purpose to deceive, whether to make money, sway opinions or cause confusion. - Example: A website invents a shocking story to generate clicks and advertising revenue. - See also: Misinformation - PT: desinformação

    E

    Echo chamber

    A space (online or offline) where we only hear opinions like our own, repeated and reinforced. The difference from a filter bubble: the bubble is created by the algorithm; the echo chamber is created by us, when we only follow people who think like us. - Example: A group chat where everyone always agrees, and anyone who disagrees ends up leaving. - PT: câmara de eco

    Emotional reasoning

    Believing something is true because we feel it's true. - Example: A video that makes us angry instantly seems believable. - Tip: When something makes you angry or afraid, that's the moment to slow down, not to share. - See also: Cognitive bias - PT: raciocínio emocional

    Evidence

    Facts, data or proof that support, or challenge, an idea. - Example: A study with thousands of participants is stronger evidence than a single personal story. - See also: Argument, Fact - PT: evidência

    F

    Fact

    Something that can be checked: measured, observed, confirmed by independent sources. - Example: "This app has 50 million users." - See also: Interpretation, Opinion - PT: facto

    Fact-checking

    Journalistic work that checks whether a claim is true, misleading or false. In Portugal: Polígrafo and Observador Fact Check. - See also: Lateral reading - PT: verificação de factos

    Fallacy

    A reasoning error that makes an argument look good when it isn't. There are many; three of the most common are ad hominem, the straw man and the false dilemma. - See also: Argument - PT: falácia

    False dilemma

    A fallacy that presents only two options, usually extreme ones, as if there were no others. - Example: "Either you quit social media, or you'll be addicted forever." - See also: Fallacy - PT: falso dilema

    Filter bubble

    When algorithms mostly show you content similar to what you already think or like, and you stop seeing other perspectives. - Example: Two people search for the same news story and get very different results. - See also: Echo chamber, Algorithm - PT: bolha de filtro

    G

    Generative AI

    Artificial intelligence that can create new content (text, images, audio, video) from instructions. It's a useful tool, but its answers need checking too. - Example: Conversational chatbots and image generators. - See also: AI hallucination, Deepfake - PT: IA generativa

    H

    Hypothesis

    A provisional explanation or idea that still needs testing. A good thinker also looks for the evidence that could prove it wrong. - Example: In the 2-4-6 challenge, "even numbers going up by 2" is a hypothesis, and it's wrong! - See also: Confirmation bias - PT: hipótese

    I

    Inference

    A conclusion we reach from clues or evidence. - Example: The ground is wet and people are carrying umbrellas: you infer that it rained. - See also: Conclusion - PT: inferência

    Interpretation

    One possible reading of the facts. Two well-informed people can interpret the same facts differently, and that's legitimate. - Example: "The app is successful because it's very addictive." (Someone else might say: "…because it's really useful.") - See also: Fact, Opinion - PT: interpretação

    L

    Lateral reading

    A technique used by professional fact-checkers: instead of spending a long time on a website trying to work out whether it's reliable, you open other tabs and see what other sources say about it. - See also: Fact-checking, Source - PT: leitura lateral

    M

    Misinformation

    False information shared without intending to deceive, for example by someone who believed it. - Example: A relative forwards a fake "health tip" because they think they're helping. - See also: Disinformation - PT: informação errada

    O

    Opinion

    What someone thinks or values about a subject. It can't be proven like a fact, but it can (and should) be backed by good arguments. - Example: "Young people should spend less time on social media." - See also: Fact, Interpretation - PT: opinião

    P

    Premise

    A statement used as a reason or starting point in an argument. An argument is only strong if the logic works and the premises are true. - Example: "Videos with millions of views are usually true." (This premise is false!) - PT: premissa

    R

    Reverse image search

    Searching with an image (instead of words) to find out where it has appeared before and in what context. Tools: Google Lens, TinEye. - Example: You discover that the photo "from yesterday" has actually been circulating since 2015. - See also: Deepfake - PT: pesquisa inversa de imagens

    S

    Source

    Where a piece of information comes from: who produced it and where it was published. - See also: Credibility, CRAAP test, Lateral reading - PT: fonte

    Sponsored content

    Content paid for by a brand, which can look like a personal opinion or a news story. By law it should be labelled (#ad, "paid partnership"). - Example: An influencer praises a supplement the brand gave them for free. - See also: CRAAP test (Purpose) - PT: conteúdo patrocinado

    Straw man

    A fallacy that distorts or exaggerates what someone said, to make it easier to attack. - Example: "You want to ban phones in class? So you want to go back to the Stone Age!" - See also: Fallacy - PT: espantalho

    Missing a word? This glossary is updated with current topics. Suggest new terms to the workshop facilitator.
