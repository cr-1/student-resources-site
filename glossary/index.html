    Glossary · Think like you care

    Key words for thinking, getting informed and debating better.

    This glossary goes with the Think like you care workshop (Pensa como quem se importa). You don't need to memorise it: come back whenever a word trips you up. Each entry has a short definition, an example, links to related terms, and the Portuguese equivalent used in the workshop slides.

    Remember: you can take a stand while remaining open to changing your mind as you learn more. Science advances by revising what we think we know. So can you.

    Jump to: A · B · C · D · E · F · G · H · I · L · M · N · O · P · R · S · U

    A

    Ad hominem

    A fallacy that attacks the person instead of answering their argument. - Example: "Don't listen to what he says about the climate: he didn't even finish secondary school." - See also: Fallacy - PT: ad hominem

    AI hallucination

    When an AI tool makes up information (a figure, a quote, a source) and presents it with total confidence, as if it were true. - Example: A chatbot recommends a book whose title and author don't exist. - See also: Generative AI - PT: alucinação (IA)

    Algorithm

    A set of rules a program follows to make a decision. On social media, the algorithm chooses what appears in your feed, usually based on what keeps you on the screen. - Example: You watched three cooking videos and now your feed is full of them. - See also: Filter bubble, Attention economy - PT: algoritmo

    Apophenia

    The tendency to see meaningful connections and patterns in things that are actually random. Our brains evolved this way because missing a real pattern (a predator in the bushes) used to cost far more than seeing one that wasn't there. - Example: "Every time I wear these trainers, my team wins." - See also: Pareidolia, Clustering illusion, Randomness - PT: apofenia

    Argument

    A set of reasons (premises) that support an idea (a conclusion). - Example: "Not sleeping enough hurts concentration. I have a test tomorrow. So I should go to bed early." - See also: Premise, Conclusion, Evidence - PT: argumento

    Attention economy

    A business model where your attention is the product: the longer you spend on an app, the more money it makes from advertising. That's why content that triggers strong emotions (outrage, fear, surprise) tends to get promoted more. - See also: Algorithm, Emotional reasoning - PT: economia da atenção

    B

    Bandwagon effect

    The tendency to believe or do something because lots of other people do. - Example: "Everyone in class already has this app, so it must be safe." - See also: Cognitive bias - PT: efeito de manada

    Barnum effect

    The tendency to accept vague descriptions that could fit anyone as if they were "made for us". Also called the Forer effect. - Example: A horoscope says "sometimes you're sociable, other times you'd rather be alone", and it feels like it knows you. - See also: Cognitive bias - PT: efeito Barnum

    Base rate

    How common something is to begin with, before you look at any test or clue. Ignoring it (base rate neglect) is one of the most common reasoning errors: when something is rare, even a good test produces many false alarms. - Example: An AI detector catches 90% of AI-written essays and wrongly flags only 5% of honest ones. If 10% of students use AI, then of every 3 students flagged, 1 is innocent. - Tip: Ask "How common is this, to start with?" and think in people, not percentages: "Out of 1000 students, how many…?" - See also: False positive, Natural frequencies - PT: taxa de base

    Birthday paradox

    In a group of just 23 people, there's a better than 50% chance that two of them share a birthday. With 30 people, it's about 70%. It feels impossible because we think "will someone share my birthday?", but the question is about any pair, and 23 people make 253 possible pairs. - Lesson: Coincidences that seem incredible are often almost inevitable when there are many chances for them to happen. - See also: Randomness - PT: paradoxo do aniversário

    C

    Calibration

    How well your confidence matches reality. If you're well calibrated, the things you say you're "90% sure" about turn out true about 90% of the time. Most people aren't: they're overconfident. The good news is that calibration can be trained, which is why weather forecasters, who get feedback every day, are so good at it. - See also: Degrees of confidence, Overconfidence - PT: calibração

    Causation

    When one thing actually produces another. Showing causation is much harder than showing correlation: scientists use controlled experiments, look for a mechanism, and check whether the result holds up in other contexts. - Example: Smoking causes lung cancer. This was established through many studies and a known biological mechanism, not from a single correlation. - See also: Correlation, Control group - PT: causalidade

    Clustering illusion

    Seeing meaningful clusters or streaks in data that are actually random. Real randomness produces clumps, runs and gaps, and our brains want to explain them. - Example: During the Second World War, Londoners believed bombs were aimed at certain neighbourhoods. A 1946 analysis showed the pattern was exactly what pure chance would produce. - See also: Randomness, Apophenia - PT: ilusão de aglomerado

    Cognitive bias

    A mental shortcut that distorts our reasoning without us noticing. Everyone has them: the goal isn't to pretend they don't exist, but to learn to recognise them. - Examples: confirmation bias, bandwagon effect, emotional reasoning, Barnum effect, survivorship bias, overconfidence. - PT: viés cognitivo

    Conclusion

    The idea an argument is trying to support. - Example: In "It's raining, so I'll take an umbrella", the conclusion is "I'll take an umbrella". - PT: conclusão

    Confirmation bias

    The tendency to look for, notice and remember mainly the information that confirms what we already think. - Example: We only follow people who think like us and ignore everyone else. In the four-card puzzle, most people turn over the card that could confirm the rule, not the one that could break it. - Tip: Ask yourself: "What would make me change my mind?" - See also: Hypothesis, Falsifiability, Filter bubble - PT: viés de confirmação

    Confounding factor

    A third factor that affects two things at once, making them look connected even though neither causes the other. - Example: Ice cream sales and drownings both go up on hot days. The heat is the confounding factor. - See also: Correlation, Reverse causation - PT: terceiro fator (ou variável de confusão)

    Control group

    In an experiment, the group that does not receive the treatment, used for comparison. Without one, almost any treatment seems to work, because people often get better anyway (see regression to the mean). The strongest experiments assign people to groups at random. - Example: To test a new study method, one randomly chosen group uses it and the other doesn't. Then you compare the results. - See also: Causation - PT: grupo de controlo

    Correlation

    When two things tend to change together. Correlation is a clue worth investigating, not proof that one thing causes the other. Before concluding, consider four explanations: chance, reverse causation, a confounding factor, or real causation. - Example: Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel Prizes. (A real correlation, published in 2012. The likely explanation is that richer countries do both.) - Tip: For absurd but real correlations, see tylervigen.com. - PT: correlação

    CRAAP test

    Five questions for evaluating a source:

    Letter Criterion Question PT
    C Currency When was it published? Is it still up to date? Atualidade
    R Relevance Does it actually answer my question? Relevância
    A Authority Who wrote it? Do they know the subject? Autoridade
    A Accuracy Is there evidence? Do other sources confirm it? Precisão
    P Purpose Is it trying to inform, sell, persuade or entertain? Propósito
    • See also: Source, Credibility
    • PT: teste CRAAP

    Credibility

    How much trust a source deserves. It depends on who's writing, what they know about the subject, the evidence they give and their intentions. - Example: An article by a medical specialist, citing studies, has more credibility on health than an anonymous comment. - PT: credibilidade

    Critical thinking

    Analysing information carefully to reach a well-founded judgement. It isn't having the right answer to everything, or being contrary: it's wanting to be well informed, enjoying putting ideas to the test, and being open to changing your mind. - PT: pensamento crítico

    D

    Deepfake

    A fake video, image or audio clip made with AI that looks real. It can make someone appear to say or do something they never said or did. - Example: A video of a public figure announcing something they never announced. - See also: Reverse image search - PT: deepfake

    Degrees of confidence

    Thinking about beliefs as percentages ("I'm about 70% sure") instead of just yes or no. It lets you take a position and still stay open: moving from 70% to 60% isn't losing an argument, it's learning. - Example: A weather forecast of "70% chance of rain" is a degree of confidence. - See also: Calibration, Updating beliefs - PT: graus de confiança

    Disinformation

    False or misleading information created on purpose to deceive, whether to make money, sway opinions or cause confusion. - Example: A website invents a shocking story to generate clicks and advertising revenue. - See also: Misinformation - PT: desinformação

    E

    Echo chamber

    A space (online or offline) where we only hear opinions like our own, repeated and reinforced. The difference from a filter bubble: the bubble is created by the algorithm; the echo chamber is created by us, when we only follow people who think like us. - Example: A group chat where everyone always agrees, and anyone who disagrees ends up leaving. - PT: câmara de eco

    Emotional reasoning

    Believing something is true because we feel it's true. - Example: A video that makes us angry instantly seems believable. - Tip: When something makes you angry or afraid, that's the moment to slow down, not to share. - See also: Cognitive bias - PT: raciocínio emocional

    Evidence

    Facts, data or proof that support, or challenge, an idea. - Example: A study with thousands of participants is stronger evidence than a single personal story. - See also: Argument, Fact - PT: evidência

    F

    Fact

    Something that can be checked: measured, observed, confirmed by independent sources. - Example: "This app has 50 million users." - See also: Interpretation, Opinion - PT: facto

    Fact-checking

    Journalistic work that checks whether a claim is true, misleading or false. In Portugal: Polígrafo and Observador Fact Check. - See also: Lateral reading - PT: verificação de factos

    Fallacy

    A reasoning error that makes an argument look good when it isn't. There are many; three of the most common are ad hominem, the straw man and the false dilemma. - See also: Argument - PT: falácia

    False dilemma

    A fallacy that presents only two options, usually extreme ones, as if there were no others. - Example: "Either you quit social media, or you'll be addicted forever." - See also: Fallacy - PT: falso dilema

    False positive

    When a test says "yes" but the true answer is "no": an innocent student flagged by an AI detector, a spam filter blocking a real email. The opposite, a false negative, is when a test misses something that is really there. - See also: Base rate - PT: falso positivo (e falso negativo)

    Falsifiability

    The idea, from the philosopher Karl Popper, that a scientific claim must be testable in a way that could prove it wrong. A good scientist doesn't only look for confirmation, but for the experiment that could refute the idea. - Example: "All swans are white" is falsifiable: one black swan would disprove it. "Everything happens for a reason" isn't: no observation could ever count against it. - See also: Hypothesis, Confirmation bias - PT: falsificabilidade (ou refutabilidade)

    Filter bubble

    When algorithms mostly show you content similar to what you already think or like, and you stop seeing other perspectives. - Example: Two people search for the same news story and get very different results. - See also: Echo chamber, Algorithm - PT: bolha de filtro

    G

    Gambler's fallacy

    Believing that after a run of one result, the opposite is now "due". A fair coin has no memory: after five heads, the next toss is still 50/50. - Example: "Red has come up six times in a row on the roulette wheel, so black has to come next." - See also: Randomness - PT: falácia do jogador

    Generative AI

    Artificial intelligence that can create new content (text, images, audio, video) from instructions. It's a useful tool, but its answers need checking too. - Example: Conversational chatbots and image generators. - See also: AI hallucination, Deepfake - PT: IA generativa

    H

    Hypothesis

    A provisional explanation or idea that still needs testing. A good thinker also looks for the evidence that could prove it wrong. - Example: In the 2-4-6 challenge, "even numbers going up by 2" is a hypothesis, and it's wrong! - See also: Confirmation bias, Falsifiability - PT: hipótese

    I

    Inference

    A conclusion we reach from clues or evidence. - Example: The ground is wet and people are carrying umbrellas: you infer that it rained. - See also: Conclusion - PT: inferência

    Interpretation

    One possible reading of the facts. Two well-informed people can interpret the same facts differently, and that's legitimate. - Example: "The app is successful because it's very addictive." (Someone else might say: "…because it's really useful.") - See also: Fact, Opinion - PT: interpretação

    L

    Lateral reading

    A technique used by professional fact-checkers: instead of spending a long time on a website trying to work out whether it's reliable, you open other tabs and see what other sources say about it. - See also: Fact-checking, Source - PT: leitura lateral

    M

    Misinformation

    False information shared without intending to deceive, for example by someone who believed it. - Example: A relative forwards a fake "health tip" because they think they're helping. - See also: Disinformation - PT: informação errada

    N

    Natural frequencies

    Expressing probabilities as counts of people or cases ("45 out of 1000") instead of percentages. It makes tricky problems much easier to understand, and doctors are trained to use it to explain test results. - Example: Instead of "5% false positives", say "of 900 honest students, 45 get flagged by mistake". - See also: Base rate - PT: frequências naturais

    O

    Opinion

    What someone thinks or values about a subject. It can't be proven like a fact, but it can (and should) be backed by good arguments. - Example: "Young people should spend less time on social media." - See also: Fact, Interpretation - PT: opinião

    Overconfidence

    Being more certain than the evidence justifies. It's one of the most robust findings in psychology, and it affects experts too. When people give ranges they're "90% sure" contain the answer, most get only 4 to 6 out of 10 right, not 9. - See also: Calibration, Degrees of confidence - PT: excesso de confiança

    P

    Pareidolia

    Seeing meaningful shapes, especially faces, in vague or random things: clouds, plug sockets, car fronts. - Example: In 1976, a photo from the Viking 1 probe seemed to show a giant face on Mars. Later, higher-resolution images showed an ordinary eroded hill. - See also: Apophenia - PT: pareidolia

    Predictive brain

    The idea, from neuroscience, that the brain doesn't simply record the world: it constantly predicts what's most likely, based on past experience, and corrects itself when it's wrong. What we perceive is largely that prediction. Illusions reveal the rules it uses. - Example: Two identical grey squares look different depending on the background around them. - PT: cérebro preditivo

    Premise

    A statement used as a reason or starting point in an argument. An argument is only strong if the logic works and the premises are true. - Example: "Videos with millions of views are usually true." (This premise is false!) - PT: premissa

    R

    Randomness

    When outcomes can't be predicted individually, even though they follow patterns overall. Real randomness looks less "random" than we expect: it's full of streaks and clusters. - Example: In 100 real coin tosses, about 8 times out of 10 there's a run of 6 or more identical results in a row. People who invent "random" sequences almost never write more than 4. - See also: Clustering illusion, Gambler's fallacy, Birthday paradox - PT: aleatoriedade (ou acaso)

    Regression to the mean

    Extreme results are partly due to luck, so the next result tends to be closer to average, whatever you do. This makes us invent false causes. - Example: Flight instructors believed criticism worked better than praise, because pilots usually improved after a bad flight. They would have improved anyway. - See also: Control group - PT: regressão à média

    Reverse causation

    When we think A causes B, but actually B causes A. - Example: "Cities with more police officers have more crime." More police are usually sent because there's more crime. - See also: Correlation, Confounding factor - PT: causa inversa

    Reverse image search

    Searching with an image (instead of words) to find out where it has appeared before and in what context. Tools: Google Lens, TinEye. - Example: You discover that the photo "from yesterday" has actually been circulating since 2015. - See also: Deepfake - PT: pesquisa inversa de imagens

    S

    Source

    Where a piece of information comes from: who produced it and where it was published. - See also: Credibility, CRAAP test, Lateral reading - PT: fonte

    Sponsored content

    Content paid for by a brand, which can look like a personal opinion or a news story. By law it should be labelled (#ad, "paid partnership"). - Example: An influencer praises a supplement the brand gave them for free. - See also: CRAAP test (Purpose) - PT: conteúdo patrocinado

    Straw man

    A fallacy that distorts or exaggerates what someone said, to make it easier to attack. - Example: "You want to ban phones in class? So you want to go back to the Stone Age!" - See also: Fallacy - PT: espantalho

    Survivorship bias

    Drawing conclusions only from the cases that "survived" and stayed visible, while forgetting the ones that didn't. - Example: "He dropped out of university and became a millionaire." For every one who did, how many dropped out and didn't? We also only remember the old songs that were good: the bad ones were forgotten. - Tip: Ask "Who's missing from this picture?" - See also: Cognitive bias - PT: viés de sobrevivência

    U

    Updating beliefs

    Adjusting how confident you are, step by step, as new evidence arrives: neither ignoring evidence that goes against you nor overreacting to a single clue. It's an intuitive version of what statisticians call Bayesian reasoning. - Example: A friend hasn't replied for three hours. Each new clue (the message shows as "read", they posted a story, someone says they're having a hard time at home) should move your guess a little, not all the way. - See also: Degrees of confidence, Base rate - PT: atualizar crenças

    New term suggestions are welcome.
